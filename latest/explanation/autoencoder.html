The autoencoder#

An ordering is not yet a smooth track, and it may be incomplete. The local-flow walk follows a single thread through the data, so it leaves out samples off to the side of its path and everything beyond a gap larger than max_dist. On the simulated stream in the stream autoencoder tutorial, the walk orders 248 of 8000 stars. An outlier-rejecting chain also leaves samples out on purpose. And any orderer gives only an order, not a curve you can evaluate. The autoencoder, from Appendix A.2 of Nibauer et al. (2022), fills both gaps. Fill gaps and fit a track with the autoencoder shows how to train it.

Two networks#

  • The encoder maps a sample’s phase-space coordinates to an ordering coordinate and a membership probability, \((x, v) \rightarrow (\gamma, p)\). It learns from the ordered samples and generalizes to the ones the orderer skipped.

  • The decoder maps \(\gamma\) back to a position, \(\gamma \rightarrow x\). It is the smooth mean track \(x(\gamma)\), which you can evaluate at any \(\gamma\).

Training#

  1. The orderer assigns \(\gamma\) over its gamma_range (\([-1, 1]\) for the default pipeline) to the samples it ordered.

  2. In the first training phase, the encoder learns to predict \(\gamma\) from phase-space coordinates, and a membership probability \(p\) that separates the stream from a background.

  3. In the second, with the encoder frozen, the decoder is fitted to a running mean of the ordered member samples’ positions, as a warm start for the track.

  4. In the third, both networks train together on reconstructing positions plus aligning the track with the velocities. The alignment weight ramps linearly from lambda_p[0] to lambda_p[1] over this phase, which keeps the track’s direction consistent with the samples’ motion.

Each phase optimizes a different objective, so the training losses can be compared only within a phase, and the returned model holds the final epoch’s weights.

A faster alternative#

A running-mean decoder, RunningMeanDecoder, can replace the trained decoder. It averages the positions of member samples within a window in \(\gamma\), needs no training, and is fast enough to evaluate inside a likelihood at every step of an MCMC. It cuts inside tight bends, by an amount set by the window size (epitrochoid running-mean tutorial).

Membership#

The encoder’s membership probability is trained as a classifier against random points, and on its own it cannot reject interlopers that the orderer threaded into the ordering. Outlier rejection with a mixture model explains why, and the mixture model that fixes it.

Interlopers at the tips#

The default pipeline orders every sample it is given, interlopers included. An interloper beyond the end of a stream lands at the end of the ordering, so the encoder learns to map it to the most extreme \(\gamma\), and the decoder bends the end of the track out to reach it. On a gapped stream of width 0.2 with 4% interlopers (the reproducer in issue #224), the two interlopers ordered last got \(\gamma\) of 0.92 and 0.94, while no member went past 0.65, and the track ran 5.3 off the true curve.

Two things guard against this.

  • track_range does not count scattered samples as the end of the stream. Each end needs the few bins of line density just inside it to be non-empty. A track that bends out to a handful of interlopers passes them one at a time, with empty bins in between, and that stretch is cut. This applies to every trained result.

  • fit_track() can order with the SOM’s outlier mixture, outlier_mixture=SOMMixture() (The self-organizing map). A sample the mixture calls background is left out of the ordering, so the encoder learns it as a non-member and the track has no reason to reach for it. On the gapped stream this left out 13 of the 16 interlopers and none of the 404 members. It is on by default. Samples that fill their own bounding box, as a mask or an image does, look like background to it, but a fit that calls most samples background keeps them all, so on a filled mask it leaves out none of the 1071 pixels.
