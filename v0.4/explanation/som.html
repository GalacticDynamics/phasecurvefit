The self-organizing map#

SOMOrderer refines an ordering with a one-dimensional self-organizing map (SOM), following Starkman et al. (2023). It is the second stage of the default pipeline. Refine an ordering with a self-organizing map shows how to use and tune it.

A string of beads#

A SOM learns a lattice of prototype vectors in phase space. Because the lattice is one-dimensional, prototypes \(i\) and \(j\) are neighbours exactly when \(|i - j| = 1\). A trained map is a string of beads threaded through the data, and the order of the beads is the order along the curve.

Each prototype is an average over many samples. So the backbone through the prototypes is much less sensitive to local noise than a walk’s step-by-step choices or an MST’s individual edges. That is why the SOM is a refinement stage: given a rough ordering, it smooths it before the fit.

Seeding the lattice#

Chained after another orderer, the SOM starts from that ordering and holds its neighbourhood narrow (at sigma_end) from the start, so it refines the ordering locally rather than re-deriving a global one.

If the earlier stage left some samples unvisited, the SOM keeps them in its arrays and gives them zero weight. They take no part in seeding or training, and they stay unvisited (-1) in the result. Since no array changes size, the chained SOM runs under jit, vmap and grad.

On its own, its only way to seed the lattice is to bin the samples along the first principal axis of the positions. That is a fair proxy for order along the curve only while the curve does not double back along that axis, up to about one turn. Beyond that the initial lattice is tangled, and training cannot untangle it: the batch algorithm refines the neighbourhood structure it is given and cannot undo a wrong global order.

Projecting the samples#

After training, every sample is placed along the map:

  1. The prototypes are joined by a centripetal Catmull-Rom spline, resampled at points equally spaced in arc length. This smooth curve is result.backbone, and calling the result interpolates along it.

  2. Each sample is assigned to its nearest backbone point under the stage’s metric.

  3. That position is refined by projecting onto the two adjacent segments in position, clamped to each segment, except at the two ends, where data beyond the ends extrapolate rather than piling up.

Steps 2 and 3 use different geometry on purpose. A metric such as AlignedMomentumDistanceMetric is not built from an inner product, so projecting onto a segment is undefined under it. The metric chooses which segment, and position chooses where on it. The result, result.chord, is a true arc length along the backbone, a continuous coordinate rather than only a ranking.

The smooth spline is also what makes the projection work in any number of dimensions. On a piecewise-linear polyline, every sample whose nearest point is a vertex gets the same arc length; the original two-dimensional method broke that tie with an angle sweep that has no analogue in higher dimensions. On a smooth curve those ties vanish.

The smoothing length#

The setting that matters is the smoothing length along the curve,

\[\sigma_{\text{phys}} = \sigma_{\text{end}} \, \frac{L}{n_{\text{prototypes}} - 1},\]

where \(L\) is the track length. The backbone is pulled towards the centre of curvature by about \(\sigma_{\text{phys}}^2 / 2R\) on a bend of radius \(R\), so bends tighter than \(\sigma_{\text{phys}}\), and arms closer together than it, are smoothed away. The two arms of a hairpin then merge into one line, and the result still looks plausible. At the defaults \(\sigma_{\text{phys}}\) is about 3% of the track. A curve that crosses itself needs a much finer lattice than a simple arc: the structure it must represent is finer than one lattice step.

Which metric it uses#

After a stage that used velocity, ordering on position alone would undo that stage’s work: at a crossing the two branches coincide in position and differ only in velocity. On an epitrochoid, a velocity-aware MST with a rank correlation of 0.94 to the truth dropped to 0.59 under a position-only SOM, and rose to 0.99 under the default. So by default the SOM follows the previous stage. After a velocity-aware stage it uses FullPhaseSpaceDistanceMetric with metric_scale = sigma_phys / (2 |v|), which separates opposite-moving branches by about the distance the lattice can resolve. On its own, or after a position-only stage, it uses SpatialDistanceMetric.

The metric must be symmetric in the two points it compares, because it answers “which prototype is this sample nearest”. The default walk metric is not: it scores forward along the direction of travel, and under it the lattice collapses towards the head of the curve.

Contamination#

The SOM has no outlier rejection of its own by default, and contamination pulls its backbone towards the contaminants. More prototypes make this worse, because they chase the interlopers. The failure is silent: the ordering’s rank correlation against the truth stays above 0.99 even at 20% contamination, while the reconstructed track, which is what you use downstream, degrades up to four times. Turning on outlier_mixture keeps the backbone on the curve (Refine an ordering with a self-organizing map).

The outlier mixture#

outlier_mixture uses the model Outlier rejection with a mixture model describes for the autoencoder, after Hogg, Bovy & Lang (2010), §3. Each sample’s position comes either from the curve or from a flat background:

\[ \mathcal{L}_n = \pi\, f(q_n) + (1 - \pi)\, \rho_{\mathrm{bg}} , \]

where \(\pi\) is the fraction of members, \(f\) the curve’s density and \(\rho_{\mathrm{bg}}\) one over the volume of the field. Batch Kohonen training is close to fitting a Gaussian mixture: Gepperth & Pfülb (2020) show that an energy-based SOM does gradient descent on an approximation of a Gaussian mixture’s log-likelihood. So the mixture is fitted by EM inside the training loop. Each epoch computes every sample’s membership \(r_n\) from the current prototypes, refits \(\pi\) and the curve’s width \(s\) in closed form, and then counts each sample by \(\omega_n r_n\) in the update. With the prototypes standing in for the track, nothing has to be ordered before membership can be computed.

The curve’s density#

\(f\) is a Gaussian tube of width \(s\) about the polyline through the prototypes. Each segment carries a share of the members, spread evenly along it. A Gaussian about each prototype would be simpler, but it is lumpy along the track wherever \(s\) is smaller than the spacing between prototypes, and samples between two prototypes then look like background: on a clean arc it rejected 45 of 300 members.

A segment’s share is the weighted fraction of all the samples it explains, refitted each epoch. With equal shares, an outlier that drew an end prototype out to itself, as one does in a plain SOM, stayed a member, because the long segment out to it still claimed its share of the members. Weighting the shares by membership instead fed back on itself, and stretches that lost members lost their share.

The tube is open at its two ends, as chord() is: past the end prototypes the end segments carry on. Early in training the wide neighbourhood draws the lattice in from the ends of the curve. With closed ends, the samples left outside looked like background, stopped pulling the ends back out, and on a clean arc half of the members were rejected.

Widening the curve early in training#

Early in training the lattice is a rough copy of the curve, and the samples scatter about it by more than they will about the final one. While the neighbourhood is wide the lattice is a smoothed copy of the curve. Chained after another orderer, the neighbourhood starts narrow, but the lattice starts from the means of bins along the earlier ordering, which cut inside the curve’s bends. So the memberships are computed with \(s^2\) widened by \((0.1\,\sigma_t\,\Delta)^2 - (0.1\,\sigma_{\mathrm{end}}\,\Delta)^2\), where \(\Delta\) is the mean spacing between prototypes and \(\sigma_t\) shrinks geometrically from \(\max(\sigma_{\mathrm{start}}, n_{\mathrm{prototypes}}/4)\) to \(\sigma_{\mathrm{end}}\) over the epochs. The widening is zero at the last epoch.

Without it, members the early lattice missed were called background and stayed lost: on a clean arc trained for 40 epochs, 256 of 300. Tied to the lattice’s own neighbourhood, which stays at sigma_end when chained, the widening was zero there, and after an MST on a near-closed loop with three lobes and 15 prototypes, 306 of 450 members were rejected. A widening of 0.17 or more let a background of 240 samples among 540 drag the backbone off at 20 epochs.

Weights#

A weight multiplies a sample’s terms in every sum, the M-steps included, and never enters its membership, the rule Density fields derives for the autoencoder. A sample of weight 2 trains exactly as two coincident copies of it.

Limits#

At 240 background samples among 540, the backbone stayed within 0.16 of the arc for 10 epochs, and failed at 40 epochs, keeping almost all the background. Chained after another orderer the SOM’s neighbourhood stays narrow, so it can’t move a lattice the earlier stage started in the background; Refine an ordering with a self-organizing map shows the setting that helps.

The background is flat over the samples’ bounding box, so it needs room outside the curve. When the samples fill that box, as the pixels of a mask or an image do, the background explains them better than the curve does. On a straight mask of half-width 0.3, the flat background beat the fitted tube by 136 in total log-likelihood, and EM ended with a member fraction of 0.33, calling 974 of the 1071 pixels background.

So a fit whose member fraction \(\pi\) ends below 0.5 keeps every sample, as if the mixture were off. The SOM’s input is meant to be a sample of a curve, and a fit that calls most of it background has fitted the box instead. On the mask this keeps all 1071 pixels. The contaminated arc ended with \(\pi\) between 0.76 and 1 at every level of background in the table of Refine an ordering with a self-organizing map, and the four streams of The autoencoder between 0.96 and 1, so none of them changed. I tried two other fixes. A flat background over a field 1.5 times wider on each axis kept every pixel of the mask, but at 120 and 240 background samples the arc’s backbone error grew from 0.038 to 0.14 and from 0.16 to 0.71. Holding \(\pi\) at 0.5 or more in the M-step still rejected 472 pixels. The cost of the rule is a field where interlopers outnumber the members: there the mixture rejects nothing.

Why perturbing the initialization gives no ensemble#

The batch algorithm is strongly contractive: each epoch replaces every prototype with a neighbourhood-weighted mean of the data, so a prototype survives only through which samples it wins. Once two ensemble members agree on their assignments, every later epoch is identical and they stay merged, so jittered initializations converge to exactly zero spread. The spread has to come from the data, by resampling it. Resampling propagates sampling noise, which is usually what is worth reporting; varying only the initial lattice measures sensitivity to the initialization, a different question.

Differences from the paper#

If you use the SOM stage in published work, please cite Starkman et al. (2023). The 1-D lattice, the equal-frequency initialization and the projection-and-ordering procedure come from its §2.2 and Appendix A. This implementation differs in these ways, so published numbers will not reproduce exactly:

  1. Batch updates replace the paper’s online update (A9)/(A10). The batch form is the fixed point of the conventional online update; it shares that fixed point but not the path to it. (A9) as printed increments by the best-matching unit’s residual w - p^(c(n)), whose equilibrium does not contain p^(k) and is not solved by the batch form; the equivalence holds under the conventional reading, whose increment is proportional to w - p^(k).

  2. The neighbourhood width shrinks geometrically during training, where (A8) uses a fixed coupling constant.

  3. The backbone is a smooth (C1) spline through the prototypes, where §2.2.2 joins them with line segments and argues against projecting onto a smooth curve, because training fits a polyline and the spline measures against a different curve. That mismatch is accepted here in exchange for the N-dimensional projection, and it is small when \(\sigma_{\text{phys}}\) is small compared with the curve’s radius.

  4. The projection works in any number of dimensions, replacing the angle sweep §2.2.2 uses in convex regions.

  5. Each sample is compared with two segments, those next to its nearest backbone point, rather than with every node and projection as step (4) of §2.2.2 specifies. This keeps the projection fast on large catalogues.

  6. There is no progenitor anchor (step (1)), so a standalone ordering has no defined direction. Chain after another orderer, or set orient_by_velocity.

  7. Velocity enters the best-matching-unit search only when chained, or when you pass a metric and scale yourself. (A7) uses all features including velocities. The scale is derived from the data rather than chosen by the user.

  8. The binning coordinate differs. The paper bins along phi_1, the longitude of its fitted great-circle frame (§2.1); here the samples are binned along the first principal axis of the positions.

  9. The outlier mixture is an addition not in the paper.
