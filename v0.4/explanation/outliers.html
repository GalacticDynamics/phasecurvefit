Outlier rejection with a mixture model#

The autoencoder’s default membership is bad at finding outliers. Stars that are plainly not on the stream survive as members, and because they survive they pull the fitted track towards themselves, worst at the ends, where there is least data to hold it in place. This page explains why, and how modelling the background fixes it. Reject outliers with the mixture model shows how to turn it on.

Why the default membership cannot reject anything#

It is trained to keep the outliers#

The encoder’s first phase trains the membership head as a classifier:

prob_ordered_penalty = masked_mean((1.0 - prob_pred_ordered) ** 2, mask)  # p -> 1
prob_random_penalty  = masked_mean(prob_pred_random ** 2, mask)           # p -> 0

The positive set is every sample the orderer visited. An MST, like any spanning structure, has no way to reject a sample: it threads outliers into the ordering along their cheapest edge. Those outliers then land in the positive set, and the network is trained to call them members. The membership head reproduces the orderer’s blind spot, and training longer does not fix it.

The negatives are too easy#

The negatives are drawn uniformly from the bounding box of positions and velocities. A uniform draw in that box almost never has a plausible pairing of position and velocity, because real stream stars have tightly correlated \((x, v)\). So the classifier reaches a low loss by learning a coarse boundary around correlated \((x, v)\), and never has to resolve the structure across the track, which is where outliers are found.

The membership never sees the residual#

What defines an outlier is its distance from the fitted track,

\[r_n = \lVert q_n - x_\theta(\gamma_\theta(w_n)) \rVert ,\]

and the decoder computes it at every step. But no gradient connects “this star is far from the track” to “lower its membership”. The membership is badly calibrated as a result: it saturates near 1 for anything near the data, so a threshold of 0.5 cuts nothing.

Modelling the background#

Instead of classifying, the mixture model describes the data. Following Hogg, Bovy & Lang (2010), §3, each star comes either from the stream or from a smooth background:

\[ \mathcal{L}_n = \pi_n \, \mathcal{N}\!\left(q_n \,;\, x_\theta(\gamma_n),\, \sigma^2(\gamma_n)\,\mathbb{I}\right) + (1 - \pi_n)\,\rho_{\mathrm{bg}} , \]

and training minimizes \(-\sum_n \log \mathcal{L}_n\).

  • \(\pi_n\) is the encoder’s membership output. It plays the role of \(1 - P_b\) in Hogg et al., except that a network predicts a weight for each star instead of one global fraction of bad data.

  • \(\mathcal{N}(\cdot)\) is the stream: a Gaussian of width \(\sigma(\gamma)\) about the decoded track.

  • \(\rho_{\mathrm{bg}}\) is a flat background density over the field. Hogg et al. use a broad Gaussian; a uniform density is the natural analogue when the field is a bounded footprint.

The gradient with respect to \(\pi_n\) now does the right thing. Close to the track the Gaussian dominates and \(\pi_n \to 1\); far from it the background dominates and \(\pi_n \to 0\). Outlier rejection comes out of the likelihood instead of a self-referential label. The crossover is set by \(\rho_{\mathrm{bg}}\), a background surface density with a physical meaning, rather than a hand-picked cut. Hogg et al. argue against sigma-clipping and hand-tuned cuts for the same reason.

With per-sample weights, each sample’s term in the sum is multiplied by its weight, and its posterior membership doesn’t change. Density fields derives this.

The encoder’s output is the prior \(\pi_n\). The posterior membership, which posterior_membership() returns, also accounts for how far the star landed from the track, and is the number to use. It uses the width capped at the stop of sigma_ceiling, the same cap as the last epoch of training. Above the cap the width network gets no gradient, so its own output there can be wider than any width the fit used.

Two ways the fit can fail#

A plain mixture likelihood has two degenerate optima, and training can reach both. Each is closed off by a schedule. A third failure, from the encoder, is covered in the next section.

Width inflation. If a few outliers sit only a few widths off the track, the likelihood prefers to widen the stream to include them over lowering their \(\pi_n\), so more training does not help. sigma_ceiling caps the width with a ceiling that shrinks during training. Early on, while the track is still poor, everything should look like a member. As the track sharpens, the ceiling tightens and stars that stay far from it are forced into the background. The width may fall below the ceiling, but not rise above it.

Membership collapse. At the start the decoded track is meaningless, so every star has a large residual, and the cheapest move is to call the whole dataset background, \(\pi_n \to 0\). Once \(\pi_n = 0\) the stream component carries no weight, no gradient reaches the decoder, the track drifts, residuals grow, and \(\pi\) stays at zero. With outliers 20 widths away, a mixture without a warm-up pushed the median member residual to about 8, against a true width of 0.15, and rejected all 400 genuine members. warmup_frac prevents this the way EM is usually initialized: it fits the track first, with membership held near 1, and only then lets the model reject stars. In the loss, \(\pi^{\mathrm{eff}}_n = 1 - w(t)\,(1 - \pi_n)\), so at \(w = 0\) it is a plain Gaussian fit to the track.

Why the encoder is frozen#

A third failure comes from the encoder. If it keeps training while the mixture is fitted, \(\gamma\) can move under the track during the warm-up, before the width and the membership have settled. The track then drifts off the stream, and once the warm-up ends the fit calls every star background. On the outlier tutorial’s data, ordered with the default pipeline, this happened on 3 of 5 training seeds.

So with mixture membership the encoder is frozen during the joint phase by default. The trunk and the \(\gamma\) head keep the weights from the first phase, which fixes \(\gamma\), and the membership head keeps training on the frozen features, so the prior can still learn which stars to reject. On the same data and seeds, none of the fits collapsed, and every one rejected all 25 interlopers.

What it does not fix#

The encoder’s negatives are still uniform in the box, so its prior \(\pi_n\) remains a weak discriminator. The mixture works anyway, because the posterior is dominated by the residual. Harder negatives, such as real stars displaced across the track or with shuffled velocities, would sharpen the prior too; that is not implemented.
